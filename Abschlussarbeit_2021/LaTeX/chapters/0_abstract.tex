\chapter*{Abstract}

Motivated by a spectral scale selection principle from Topological Data Analysis, this thesis develops an analogous method for bandwidth selection in kernel density estimation. Kernel density estimates are computed on a grid of candidate bandwidths, ranging from geometric to linear spacing. Pairwise divergences between the estimates are transformed into a scale similarity matrix, and the selected bandwidth is the one at the largest entry of its Perron vector. By Schoenberg's theorem, several common divergences combined with completely monotone transformations yield positive semi-definite similarity matrices. For the $L^2$ divergence and the Gaussian kernel, the raw divergence matrix is a Robinson matrix whose Perron vector favors boundary scales, which motivates an exponential similarity transformation. Numerical experiments with Gaussian data reveal characteristic limiting behavior, which is then explained theoretically. The main result shows that, as the grid is refined and the transformation sharpened at a suitable rate, the spectrally selected bandwidth converges to the global minimizers of a sample-dependent energy functional. This functional measures how quickly the density estimate changes with the bandwidth. The existence of local extrema of this functional is characterized. For the Gaussian kernel, the minimizer of the leading-term approximation of its expected value is asymptotically equivalent to the MISE-optimal bandwidth on a nearly linear grid. Runtime comparisons show that minimizing the energy functional directly is more efficient, which suggests the spectral approach is mainly of interest where this functional is less tractable.
